How irregular-hours holiday pay works in the UK: the 12.07% rule

For zero-hours, casual, and part-year workers in Great Britain. This guide explains the rule, shows the maths with worked examples, and lists the mistakes that cost workers holiday pay. It covers the statutory minimum only. A contract that gives more holiday than the minimum wins over anything written here.

Who counts as an irregular-hours or part-year worker

An irregular-hours worker is someone whose paid hours are wholly or mostly variable in each pay period. A part-year worker is someone who is only required to work for part of the year, for example term-time only staff.

These two groups use the 12.07% method described below, for leave years beginning on or after 1 April 2024, in Great Britain. Workers with regular fixed hours do not use this method. They use pro-rating instead: days worked per week multiplied by 5.6, capped at 28 days. The 2024 reforms do not apply in Northern Ireland.

Where the 12.07% comes from

Every worker in the UK is entitled to a statutory minimum of 5.6 weeks of paid holiday a year. For a worker on a 5-day week, that is 28 days.

A year has 52 weeks. Take away the 5.6 weeks of holiday, and 46.4 working weeks remain. Divide the holiday by the working weeks: 5.6 ÷ 46.4 = 0.1207, or 12.07%. That is the whole secret of the number. Irregular-hours and part-year workers accrue holiday at 12.07% of the hours they work.

How accrual works each pay period

Accrual is calculated separately for each pay period, then added up. The formula for one pay period is: hours worked in the pay period × 12.07%, rounded to the nearest hour. Round up if the fraction is 0.5 or more, down if it is less. Rounding happens per period, not once at the end of the year. This matters, and it is the most common source of small errors.

Worked example: one monthly pay period

Jill works irregular hours and is paid monthly. In June she works 68 hours. 68 × 12.07% = 8.2076 hours, rounded to 8 hours. At £13 an hour, those 8 hours are worth £104.00 when the leave is taken.

Worked example: why per-period rounding matters

Two weekly pay periods of 30 hours and 15 hours: 30 × 12.07% = 3.621 hours, rounded to 4 hours; 15 × 12.07% = 1.8105 hours, rounded to 2 hours; total 6 hours. Rounding once on the combined 45 hours would give 45 × 12.07% = 5.4315, rounded to 5 hours. The correct total is 6 hours, not 5. Always round each pay period separately.

Rolled-up holiday pay, and how to check it on a payslip

Instead of paying holiday pay when leave is taken, an employer may pay rolled-up holiday pay: 12.07% of total pay, added to every payslip, for irregular-hours and part-year workers in Great Britain, for leave years beginning on or after 1 April 2024.

Three conditions must all hold: it must be at least 12.07% of total pay for the period; it must be paid at the same time as wages; and it must be shown as a separate, clearly labelled line on the payslip. It must not be hidden inside a higher hourly rate.

Rolled-up pay is paid instead of paying for leave when it is taken, not as well as it. If you are paid rolled-up holiday pay, do not add the value of accrued leave on top of it for the same leave, or you will double-count.

Worked example: rolled-up amount

Total pay for June is £884. £884 × 12.07% = £106.70 rolled-up holiday pay.

Worked example: checking a payslip

Total pay in the period is £800. The payslip shows a holiday line of £80. Expected rolled-up holiday pay = £800 × 12.07% = £96.56. £96.56 − £80 = £16.56 short. Ask payroll to show the calculation. If the payslip shows no separate holiday line at all, ask payroll for a breakdown showing the holiday element separately, and ask for the calculation in writing. Keep your hours records and payslips.

Pay for leave taken, using the 52-week average

When an irregular-hours worker takes leave, and is not on rolled-up pay, holiday pay is based on average pay over the previous 52 paid weeks. Add the pay for the previous 52 weeks in which pay was actually received, and divide by 52. That average weekly pay is the pay for a week of leave. Weeks with no pay are skipped, not counted as zero.

Common mistakes

  • Rounding once at the end of the year. Entitlement accrues per pay period and is rounded per pay period. Rounding once at the end usually gives a slightly lower total, as the 30-hour plus 15-hour example shows: 6 hours, not 5.
  • Hiding rolled-up pay inside the hourly rate. Rolled-up holiday pay must appear as a separate, clearly labelled line on the payslip. A higher hourly rate with no separate line does not meet the rule.
  • Using the 12.07% method for regular-hours staff. Workers with regular fixed hours use pro-rating (days per week × 5.6, capped at 28 days), not 12.07%. Using 12.07% for them is the wrong method.
  • Assuming the leave year is the calendar year. The 12.07% method and lawful rolled-up pay apply from the first leave year beginning on or after 1 April 2024. Until a leave year renews, the previous rules applied. If you are unsure when your leave year starts, check your contract or ask your employer. Do not assume it starts on 1 January.
  • Double-counting rolled-up pay and accrued leave. Rolled-up pay replaces pay for leave taken. Counting both for the same leave counts the same holiday twice.

Questions people ask

How is holiday pay calculated for irregular hours?

At 12.07% of the hours worked in each pay period, rounded to the nearest hour per period, for leave years beginning on or after 1 April 2024 in Great Britain.

What is the formula for calculating holiday pay?

For irregular-hours and part-year workers: hours worked in the pay period × 12.07%. For pay for leave taken: average weekly pay over the previous 52 paid weeks.

How do you calculate your holiday hours?

Add up 12.07% of the hours you worked in each pay period, rounding each period to the nearest hour, then add the rounded figures together.

What is the formula for calculating holidays?

The statutory minimum is 5.6 weeks a year. For irregular-hours and part-year workers, that converts to 12.07% of hours worked, because 5.6 ÷ (52 − 5.6) = 12.07%.

What are irregular workers?

Workers whose paid hours are wholly or mostly variable in each pay period, as opposed to workers with regular fixed hours.

Does holiday pay count towards regular hours?

No. Holiday pay is payment for time off. It is not extra hours worked, and accrued holiday hours are not added to the hours you worked in a period when working out the next period's accrual.
