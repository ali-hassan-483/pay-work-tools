How the maths works

Step 1 — Self-employment tax. Net self-employment income × 92.35% = the self-employment earnings base. Social Security tax is 12.4% of that base, up to the 2026 wage base of $184,500. Medicare tax is 2.9% of the whole base, with no cap. The additional 0.9% Medicare tax applies to the base above $200,000 (single / head of household), $250,000 (married filing jointly) or $125,000 (married filing separately), assuming no W-2 Medicare wages — if you also have W-2 wages, the threshold is shared. Half of the self-employment tax is deductible when working out income tax. No self-employment tax is due if net earnings are under $400.

Step 2 — Income tax. Adjusted gross income = net self-employment income + other income − half of self-employment tax. Taxable income = adjusted gross income − standard or itemized deduction − any QBI deduction you entered. Income tax is then worked out bracket by bracket at 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35% and 37% — only the slice of income inside each bracket is taxed at that bracket's rate.

Step 3 — Quarterly payment. Total tax = income tax + self-employment tax. Subtract credits and withholding to get the amount estimated payments must cover, then divide by 4. That assumes income is spread evenly through the year. If your income is lumpy, the IRS annualized income installment method (Form 2210, Schedule AI) may give a lower payment for an early quarter.

Worked examples (tax year 2026, standard deduction, no credits or withholding)

Example 1 — Single freelancer, $80,000 net self-employment income. Self-employment base $73,880.00; Social Security $9,161.12; Medicare $2,142.52; self-employment tax $11,303.64. Half deductible: $5,651.82. Adjusted gross income $74,348.18; taxable income $58,248.18; income tax $7,526.60. Total tax $18,830.24; quarterly payment $4,707.56.

Example 2 — Safe harbor. Last year's total tax $12,000 and last year's AGI not over $150,000: safe harbor is $12,000 for the year, or $3,000 per quarter. Paying that in four equal payments avoids an underpayment penalty even if this year's tax is higher, though a balance may still be due at filing.

Example 3 — Single freelancer, $250,000 net self-employment income. Self-employment base $230,875.00; Social Security capped at $22,878.00; Medicare $6,695.38; additional Medicare $277.88; self-employment tax $29,851.26. Income tax $46,527.80. Total tax $76,379.06; quarterly payment $19,094.77.

Limitations

  • Federal tax only. State estimated tax is separate.
  • Uses the standard deduction unless you enter an itemized amount. It does not model retirement contributions (SEP IRA, Solo 401(k)), the self-employed health insurance deduction, or most credits beyond the single credits figure you enter.
  • Additional Medicare tax here assumes no W-2 Medicare wages. If you have both W-2 and self-employment income, the $200,000 / $250,000 / $125,000 threshold is shared across both.
  • Equal quarterly payments assume even income. Uneven income needs Form 2210, Schedule AI, or you may overpay early quarters.
  • Figures are the 2026 federal figures (IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-32). The IRS updates brackets, the wage base and the standard deduction every year.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to pay quarterly, or can I just pay at filing?

If you expect to owe $1,000 or more after withholding and credits, the IRS generally requires quarterly payments. Paying only at filing can mean an underpayment penalty plus interest, even if you pay the full balance with your return.

What if my income drops mid-year?

Re-run the estimate with the lower annual figure and reduce later quarters. The safe harbor based on last year's tax still protects you from a penalty if you paid it in full, but you are not forced to keep paying last year's amount if this year's tax is genuinely lower — the 90% of this year's tax safe harbor is the other route.

Is withholding treated differently from estimated payments?

Yes. Withholding is treated as paid evenly through the year no matter when it was actually withheld, which is why a W-2 job can cover a late-year freelance windfall. Estimated payments count only from the date they are actually paid, so a missed quarter cannot be fixed by overpaying later without a possible penalty for that quarter.

Do I need to file anything with each payment?

No return is filed quarterly. You send the payment with a Form 1040-ES voucher, or pay online. You reconcile everything on your annual return.

What if I already missed a quarter?

Pay as soon as you can. The penalty is calculated per quarter on the shortfall, so paying late stops it growing, but it does not erase the penalty for the period already missed. Form 2210 works it out, and it can also waive or reduce it in some cases, for example for uneven income.

Is this tax advice?

No. This is an estimate for planning. Tax situations vary — credits, retirement plans, health insurance, rental income and state tax can all change the answer. Check IRS Form 1040-ES and Publication 505, or speak to a tax professional, before relying on a figure for filing.
