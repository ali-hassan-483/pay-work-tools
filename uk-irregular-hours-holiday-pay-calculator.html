How the maths works

Why 12.07%? Statutory holiday is 5.6 weeks a year. A year has 52 weeks, so 52 − 5.6 = 46.4 working weeks. 5.6 ÷ 46.4 = 0.1207, or 12.07%.

Step 1 — Accrual per pay period. Hours worked in the pay period × 12.07%. Round to the nearest hour, up if the fraction is 0.5 or more. Do this for each pay period separately, then add the rounded figures.

Step 2 — Value of accrued holiday. Rounded accrued hours × hourly rate. This is the value of the time off when it is taken, before any rolled-up payment is taken into account.

Step 3 — Rolled-up holiday pay. Total pay for the period × 12.07%. Rolled-up pay is paid instead of paying for leave when it is taken, not as well as it. If you are paid rolled-up holiday pay, do not add the Step 2 value on top of it for the same leave, or you will double-count.

Step 4 — 52-week average (leave taken, no rolled-up pay). Add the pay for the previous 52 paid weeks and divide by 52 (or by the number of paid weeks entered, if fewer). That average weekly pay is the pay for a week of leave. Divide it by average hours per week to get an implied hourly rate for leave taken in hours.

Worked examples

Worked example 1 — GOV.UK accrual example

Jill works irregular hours and is paid monthly. In June she works 68 hours. Accrual = 68 × 12.07% = 8.2076 hours, rounded to 8 hours. At £13 an hour, those 8 hours are worth £104.00 when taken. If her total pay for June was £884, rolled-up holiday pay would be £884 × 12.07% = £106.70.

Worked example 2 — Weekly, 30 hours

30 hours in a weekly pay period: 30 × 12.07% = 3.621 hours, rounded to 4 hours. At £12.50 an hour, that is £50.00. The leave can be taken from the next pay period.

Worked example 3 — Why per-period rounding matters

Two weekly periods of 30 hours and 15 hours accrue 4 hours and 2 hours — 6 hours in total. Rounding once on the combined 45 hours would give 5 hours. Always round each pay period separately.

Worked example 4 — Rolled-up payslip check

Total pay £800 in the period, payslip shows £80. Expected rolled-up holiday pay = £800 × 12.07% = £96.56, so the worker is £16.56 short. Ask payroll to show the calculation.

What this calculator does not do

  • It does not handle workers with regular fixed hours, who use pro-rating (days worked × 5.6, capped at 28 days), not the 12.07% method.
  • It does not handle Northern Ireland, where the 2024 Great Britain reforms do not apply.
  • It does not decide which payments count towards “normal” holiday pay in a disputed case — commission, regular overtime, and status payments can all matter. Keep payslips and ask payroll what was included.
  • It does not handle contractual holiday above the statutory minimum, carry-over rules, sickness or family leave, or pay on leaving where the contract has its own formula. A contract that gives more than the statutory minimum wins.
  • It does not deduct tax, National Insurance, pension, or student-loan deductions. Holiday pay shown here is gross.
  • Bank holidays are not listed by date here. The statutory 5.6 weeks can include bank holidays unless the contract says more; the number of bank holidays differs by nation (England and Wales 8, Scotland 9, Northern Ireland 10 in a typical year). Check GOV.UK bank-holiday dates for the year in question.

Frequently asked questions

Who can use the 12.07% method?

In Great Britain, irregular-hours workers — where paid hours are wholly or mostly variable in each pay period — and part-year workers, for leave years beginning on or after 1 April 2024. Workers with regular fixed hours use pro-rating instead. The 2024 reforms do not apply in Northern Ireland.

What is rolled-up holiday pay, and is it legal?

Holiday pay paid with every payslip at 12.07% of total pay, instead of when leave is taken. For irregular-hours and part-year workers in Great Britain it is lawful for leave years beginning on or after 1 April 2024. It must be at least 12.07%, paid at the same time as wages, and shown as a separate, clearly labelled line on the payslip. It must not be hidden inside a higher hourly rate.

Does 5.6 weeks include bank holidays?

The statutory minimum can include bank and public holidays unless the contract gives more. An employer can require statutory leave to be taken on bank holidays if the contract allows it. If your contract gives more than 5.6 weeks, the extra days are contractual — check how they are treated.

What if my leave year started before 1 April 2024?

The new accrual method and lawful rolled-up pay apply from the first leave year beginning on or after 1 April 2024. Until your leave year renews, the previous rules applied. If you are unsure when your leave year starts, check your contract or ask your employer — do not assume it is the calendar year.

Why round each pay period separately?

Because entitlement accrues per pay period. Rounding each period to the nearest hour and then adding them can give a different, usually slightly higher, total than rounding once at the end. The 30-hour plus 15-hour example in the worked examples shows 6 hours, not 5.

My payslip does not show a separate holiday line. What should I do?

Ask payroll for a breakdown showing the holiday element separately. Keep your hours records and payslips. If the amount is short or the line is missing, and payroll cannot explain it, contact Acas or Citizens Advice. Do not rely on a verbal assurance — ask for the calculation in writing.
