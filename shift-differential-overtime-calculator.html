How the maths works

Step 1 — Differential per hour. Flat amount: the $ amount you entered. Percentage: base rate × percentage ÷ 100.

Step 2 — Straight-time earnings. (Base rate × total hours) + (differential per hour × differential-shift hours).

Step 3 — Blended regular rate. Straight-time earnings ÷ total hours. Under the FLSA, a shift differential must generally be folded into the regular rate before overtime is calculated, under 29 CFR 778.207. It is not correct, as a general rule, to calculate overtime on the base rate alone and then add the differential as if it were separate.

Step 4 — Overtime premium. Federal: 0.5 × regular rate for each hour over 40 in the week. California: 0.5 × regular rate for overtime hours, plus a further 1 × regular rate for double-time hours, because those hours were already counted once at the regular rate in straight-time earnings.

Step 5 — Gross pay. Straight-time earnings + overtime premium + double-time premium.

Worked examples

Worked example 1 — Federal, all hours on nights

Base $20, $2 night differential, 10 hours Monday to Friday = 50 hours, all on nights, Federal mode.

Straight-time earnings = ($20 × 50) + ($2 × 50) = $1,100. Blended regular rate = $1,100 ÷ 50 = $22. Overtime premium = 0.5 × $22 × 10 = $110. Gross = $1,210. If overtime had been calculated on the $20 base rate only, with the differential paid straight, gross would be $1,200 — $10 less.

Worked example 2 — California, one long day

Base $20, no differential, 14 hours in one day, California mode. Regular 8 hours, overtime 4 hours (over 8 up to 12), double time 2 hours (over 12). Gross = (8 × $20) + (4 × $30) + (2 × $40) = $360, even if the week total is under 40 hours.

What this calculator does not do

  • It does not handle bonuses, commissions, on-call pay, holiday premiums, or other pay that may also affect the regular rate.
  • It does not handle alternative workweek schedules, exemptions, agricultural rules, or the 8-and-80 system.
  • It assumes the differential applies at one rate for all differential hours. If you have two different differentials in one week, calculate each part separately or use the blended method with total differential pay.
  • It does not deduct tax, pension, or other deductions. Gross pay is before deductions.

Frequently asked questions

Does the FLSA require a shift differential?

No. A differential is a matter of agreement between employer and employee. The FLSA does not require extra pay for night, weekend or holiday work as such. Once a differential is paid, it generally must be included in the regular rate for overtime.

What if only some hours were night shift?

Enter only those hours in “Hours worked on the differential shift”. The calculator blends the rate: total straight-time earnings divided by total hours. The regular rate therefore changes each week depending on how many differential hours you worked.

What is the difference between a flat $ differential and a % differential?

A flat $ amount stays the same when your base rate rises. A % differential rises with your base rate. Over time, after raises, a % differential usually keeps more of its value. Model both when comparing job offers.

When does California double time apply?

Generally after 12 hours in a workday, and after 8 hours on the seventh consecutive day of work in a workweek. The first 8 hours on the seventh day are generally at 1.5×. Exemptions and alternative workweek schedules change this, so check the DIR page linked above.

Why is my payslip different from this result?

Common reasons: bonuses or other premiums included in the real regular rate, a different workweek definition, an alternative workweek schedule, rounding, or your employer calculating overtime on the base rate only. Keep this breakdown and ask payroll to show the regular rate they used.

Can I use this for salaried or exempt workers?

No. This tool is for non-exempt hourly workers. Exempt, salaried, and special-category workers have different rules. If you are unsure whether you are exempt, check with your state labour department.
