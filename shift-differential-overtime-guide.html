How shift differential affects overtime pay: the FLSA blended rate, plus California

For hourly, shift, night, and weekend workers in the US. This guide explains how a shift differential changes your overtime rate, how California's daily rules differ from the federal weekly rule, and the mistakes that leave night workers underpaid. It covers the general rules only. Exemptions, union contracts, and alternative workweek schedules can change the answer for a particular worker.

What a shift differential is, and why the FLSA does not require one

A shift differential is extra pay per hour for working hours most people would rather not work: nights, evenings, weekends, or holidays. It is usually stated in one of two ways: a flat amount, for example $2.00 per hour, or a percentage of the base rate, for example 10% of base pay.

No federal law requires an employer to pay a shift differential. It is a matter of agreement between employer and employee, or of a union contract. But once a differential is paid, federal law does govern how it interacts with overtime, and that is where most of the money is lost or found.

How the blended regular rate works, step by step

Under the Fair Labor Standards Act, overtime is paid at 1.5 times the "regular rate of pay", and the regular rate legally includes shift differentials. The steps are:

Step 1 — Differential per hour. Flat amount: the dollar amount entered. Percentage: base rate × percentage ÷ 100.

Step 2 — Straight-time earnings. (Base rate × total hours) + (differential per hour × differential-shift hours).

Step 3 — Blended regular rate. Straight-time earnings ÷ total hours.

Step 4 — Overtime premium. Federal: 0.5 × regular rate for each hour over 40 in the week. The 0.5, not 1.5, is the premium, because the straight-time earnings in Step 2 already counted those hours once at the regular rate.

Step 5 — Gross pay. Straight-time earnings + overtime premium (+ double-time premium in California, where it applies).

The key point: the differential raises the regular rate itself, so it raises the overtime rate too. It is not a side payment added after overtime is calculated.

Federal overtime: time and a half on the blended rate, not the base rate

Worked example: federal, all hours on nights

Base $20, $2 night differential, 10 hours Monday to Friday = 50 hours, all on nights, federal rules. Straight-time earnings = ($20 × 50) + ($2 × 50) = $1,100. Blended regular rate = $1,100 ÷ 50 = $22. Overtime premium = 0.5 × $22 × 10 = $110. Gross = $1,210.

If overtime had been calculated on the $20 base rate only, with the differential paid straight, gross would be $1,200, which is $10 less. That $10 a week is $520 a year, from this one error alone, before any other premium is considered.

California: daily overtime, double time, and the 7th consecutive day

California is stricter than federal law, because overtime applies daily as well as weekly. Under California Labor Code § 510, for non-exempt workers on a standard schedule: hours 1–8 in a workday at the regular rate; hours 8–12 in a workday at 1.5× the regular rate; hours over 12 in a workday at 2× the regular rate (double time); and on the 7th consecutive workday in a workweek, the first 8 hours at 1.5× and hours over 8 at 2×.

California overtime can therefore be owed even when the week's total is under 40 hours.

Worked example: California, one long day

Base $20, no differential, 14 hours in one day, California rules. Regular 8 hours, overtime 4 hours (over 8 up to 12), double time 2 hours (over 12). Gross = (8 × $20) + (4 × $30) + (2 × $40) = $360, even if the week total is under 40 hours.

Worked example: California, 7th consecutive day

Base $20, no differential, 10 hours on the 7th consecutive day of work in a workweek. First 8 hours at 1.5×, remaining 2 hours at 2×. Gross for that day = (8 × $30) + (2 × $40) = $320.

Common mistakes

  • Calculating overtime on the base rate only. The most expensive mistake. The differential must generally be folded into the regular rate before overtime is calculated, under 29 CFR 778.207. Overtime at exactly 1.5 × base rate, for a worker who regularly earns a differential, is a warning sign. Keep the breakdown and ask payroll to show the regular rate they used.
  • Treating the differential as a separate bonus. A differential is not a bonus paid on top of a finished overtime calculation. It is part of straight-time earnings, and therefore part of the regular rate.
  • Thinking California overtime is weekly only. California has daily overtime and double time. A single 14-hour day earns overtime and double time even in a short week.
  • Using the tool for salaried or exempt workers. This guide and the calculator are for non-exempt hourly workers. Exempt, salaried, and special-category workers have different rules. If you are unsure whether you are exempt, check with your state labour department.
  • Comparing flat and percentage differentials without modelling both. A flat dollar amount stays the same when the base rate rises. A percentage differential rises with the base rate, so after raises it usually keeps more of its value. Model both when comparing job offers.

Questions people ask

Does a shift differential increase my overtime pay?

Yes. Under the FLSA it is folded into the regular rate before overtime is calculated, so overtime is 1.5 times the blended rate, not 1.5 times the base rate.

Does the FLSA require a shift differential?

No. A differential is a matter of agreement between employer and employee. The FLSA does not require extra pay for night, weekend, or holiday work as such. Once a differential is paid, it generally must be included in the regular rate for overtime.

What is the difference between a flat dollar differential and a percentage differential?

A flat amount stays the same when your base rate rises. A percentage differential rises with your base rate. Over time, after raises, a percentage differential usually keeps more of its value.

When does California double time apply?

Generally after 12 hours in a workday, and after 8 hours on the seventh consecutive day of work in a workweek. The first 8 hours on the seventh day are generally at 1.5×. Exemptions and alternative workweek schedules change this.

Why is my payslip different from this result?

Common reasons: bonuses or other premiums included in the real regular rate, a different workweek definition, an alternative workweek schedule, rounding, or an employer calculating overtime on the base rate only. Keep the breakdown and ask payroll to show the regular rate they used.

Can I use this for salaried or exempt workers?

No. This guide is for non-exempt hourly workers. Exempt, salaried, and special-category workers have different rules.
